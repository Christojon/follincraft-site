Sleep better in a tent: fix what wakes you up

Start with the thing that woke you up. A sore hip, a cold back and a wet sleeping bag need different fixes. Buying a warmer bag will not flatten the ground or put your headlamp within reach.

By Chris Follin · Camp field guide

Do the lying-down test

Before the vehicle is unpacked, place the pad where you intend to sleep. Lie in your normal sleeping position with the pillow and bedding you brought. Roll over. If you slide, bottom out or hit the tent wall, there is still daylight to fix it.

Choose an established, permitted surface. Move the sleeping position or select another suitable site; do not dig, flatten vegetation or build a new pad to rescue a poor spot.

What woke you up?

Start with one observation. These are equipment checks, not guarantees that a particular setup will keep you comfortable.

Cold along your back or underside
Check pad insulation, separately from the warmth above you.
Hip or shoulder presses into the ground
Check support while lying down, then whether the pad holds its inflation.
A wet patch on the bag
Trace where the moisture came from before blaming the whole tent.
You keep sliding toward one end
Recheck the sleeping surface and placement. Use another suitable established spot if needed; extra bedding will not level the ground.

Start with the symptom

Cold underneath, warm on top

Check the pad before adding another blanket above you. Its insulation matters separately from its thickness. Therm-a-Rest explains that R-value describes resistance to heat flow—not a guaranteed minimum temperature. Match the pad and bedding to the expected conditions and your own needs; do not treat the number on a bag as a promise of comfortable sleep. Read the pad-insulation explanation and sleeping-bag rating guidance.

For the next trip, record the pad model, bedding, forecast low and where you felt cold. That gives you a specific change to evaluate. This page does not claim a tested temperature limit for a particular combination.

Hip or shoulder pressure

On safe, clean ground, adjust an inflatable pad a little at a time while lying in your normal position, within its instructions. Sitting upright puts pressure in a different place and is a poor comfort test. If your hip reaches the ground, letting out more air is not the fix. If the pad loses support repeatedly, investigate a leak before buying thicker bedding.

Check pillow height in the same position. A pillow that feels right on a mattress at home can hold your neck at a different angle on a narrow camp pad. Change one thing, lie down again, then decide.

Damp bedding

Separate water entering the tent from moisture forming inside it. Check whether the bag touches a wet wall, wet clothes came into the bedding, or a bottle leaked. Keep the fly and inner fabric separated as the tent maker directs, use its vents, and keep wet outer gear away from the bed. Ventilation can help; it cannot guarantee a dry tent in every condition.

Finish these jobs before bed

Check the actual bed, not just the bags you carried into the tent. Ticks are not saved by the site.

Ready for the night?

Change one thing next time

Record the symptom before making a shopping list. Here is an example you can copy into your trip notes:

What happened?
Woke at 3 a.m.; hip on ground; pad softer than at bedtime.
What was the setup?
Record pad model, bedding, sleeping position and forecast low. Distinguish the forecast from any temperature you actually measured.
What will I check first?
Test whether the pad holds inflation at home, following its instructions, before buying thicker bedding.

Dry the tent and bedding fully at home. If you cannot stay warm and dry during the trip, use a safer shelter or end the overnight instead of trying to endure it.
